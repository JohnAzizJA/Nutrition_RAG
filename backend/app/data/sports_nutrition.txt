Sports Nutrition: Fueling Performance, Recovery, and Body Composition

PRE-WORKOUT NUTRITION
The goal of pre-workout nutrition is to maximize energy availability, preserve muscle glycogen, and minimize muscle protein breakdown during training.

Timing: eat a balanced meal 2–3 hours before training. If training within 1 hour, have a smaller, easily digestible snack.

What to eat pre-workout:
- Carbohydrates (primary fuel): complex carbs 2–3 hours out; simple carbs 30–60 minutes out if needed
- Moderate protein: 20–30 g to reduce muscle breakdown
- Low fat and fiber immediately pre-workout (slows digestion and can cause GI discomfort)

Pre-workout meal examples (2–3 hours before):
- Rice with chicken and vegetables
- Oats with a banana and milk
- Whole grain bread with eggs and avocado
- Ful medames with aysh baladi (excellent Egyptian pre-workout option — complex carbs + plant protein)

Pre-workout snack examples (30–60 min before):
- Banana with a teaspoon of peanut butter
- Greek yogurt with honey
- A date and a glass of milk
- Rice cake with banana

Caffeine: 3–6 mg per kg of body weight 30–60 minutes before training improves endurance, strength, and focus. Coffee, tea, or pre-workout supplements. Avoid caffeine within 6 hours of sleep.

POST-WORKOUT NUTRITION
The post-workout window (1–2 hours after training) is when muscles are most receptive to nutrients for repair and growth. Delaying post-workout nutrition by more than 2 hours slows recovery.

The anabolic window: while the 30-minute post-workout protein window was overstated in older research, consuming protein within 2 hours of training is still important. Total daily protein intake matters most.

Post-workout priorities:
1. Protein: 20–40 g of high-quality protein to initiate muscle protein synthesis. Leucine is the key amino acid trigger (present in animal proteins, whey, soy, eggs).
2. Carbohydrates: 0.5–1.5 g per kg of body weight to replenish muscle glycogen. More important after endurance exercise than strength training.
3. Hydration: replace fluids lost during exercise.

Post-workout meal examples:
- Grilled chicken with rice and vegetables
- Eggs with whole grain toast
- Protein shake with milk and a banana
- Tuna with whole wheat pita
- Greek yogurt with oats and berries
- Grilled farouj (chicken) with Egyptian rice — excellent post-workout Egyptian meal

HYDRATION FOR PERFORMANCE
Dehydration of even 1–2% of body weight impairs strength, endurance, and cognitive function.

During exercise hydration:
- Drink 400–600 ml of water 2 hours before exercise
- Drink 150–250 ml every 15–20 minutes during exercise
- After exercise: drink 1.5× the fluid lost (weigh yourself before and after; each kg lost = 1 liter of fluid to replace)

Electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium) are lost in sweat. For sessions under 60 minutes, water is sufficient. For longer or more intense sessions, sports drinks or electrolyte supplements help maintain performance and prevent cramping.

Signs of dehydration: dark urine (target pale yellow), thirst, decreased performance, headache, dizziness.

PROTEIN SUPPLEMENTS
Whole food protein sources are always preferred. Supplements are convenient when whole food intake is inadequate.

Whey protein: derived from milk. Fast-digesting; ideal post-workout. Contains all essential amino acids. 80% protein by weight in concentrate; 90% in isolate.

Casein protein: slow-digesting (digests over 5–7 hours). Best taken before bed to reduce overnight muscle breakdown.

Plant-based proteins: pea protein, rice protein, soy protein. Soy is a complete protein. Combining pea + rice provides a complete amino acid profile similar to whey.

Creatine monohydrate: the most researched and effective legal supplement for strength and power sports. Increases phosphocreatine stores, enabling more ATP production during short, intense efforts. 3–5 g per day, taken consistently (timing doesn't matter). No loading phase required. Safe for long-term use. Does not meaningfully cause hair loss in the vast majority of users (>95%); one small rugby study measured DHT elevation — not hair loss directly — under an aggressive loading protocol, and has not been replicated. Those with strong androgenetic alopecia predisposition may want to monitor, but there is no established causal link.

Beta-alanine: buffers lactic acid during high-intensity exercise; may extend endurance at 3–6 minute effort durations. 3.2–6.4 g/day. Causes harmless tingling (paresthesia).

Caffeine: 3–6 mg/kg pre-workout. Most evidence-supported ergogenic aid after creatine.

BCAAs: branched-chain amino acids (leucine, isoleucine, valine). Unnecessary if protein intake is adequate (1.6+ g/kg/day). May reduce soreness. Not worth the cost if diet is sufficient.

NUTRITION PERIODIZATION
Match nutrition to training phase:
- Strength/hypertrophy phase: calorie surplus (200–300 kcal above maintenance), high protein (2.0 g/kg), adequate carbs to fuel training
- Fat loss/cutting phase: calorie deficit (300–500 below maintenance), very high protein (2.2–2.5 g/kg) to preserve muscle, lower carbs
- Maintenance/recomposition: maintenance calories, 1.8–2.0 g/kg protein, regular resistance training

Calorie cycling: eating more on training days (surplus) and less on rest days (deficit) — effective for simultaneously building muscle and losing fat over time.

MUSCLE SORENESS (DOMS)
Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness (DOMS) peaks 24–72 hours after training, especially after novel exercises or high-volume sessions. It is caused by eccentric muscle damage, not lactic acid (which clears within minutes of exercise).

Managing DOMS:
- Adequate post-workout nutrition (protein + carbs)
- Light active recovery (walking, gentle cycling)
- Cold water immersion or contrast showers
- Adequate sleep
- Foam rolling (moderate evidence; reduces perceived soreness)
- Anti-inflammatory foods: tart cherry juice, turmeric, omega-3s
- Return to training — movement accelerates recovery

BODY COMPOSITION TARGETS FOR ATHLETES
- Male athletes: body fat 10–20% for general fitness; 6–12% for aesthetic sports; 15–25% for strength sports
- Female athletes: 18–28% for general fitness; 14–20% for aesthetic sports; 20–30% for strength sports
- Below 6% (men) or 14% (women) body fat carries health risks including hormonal disruption and reduced immune function

NUTRITION FOR ENDURANCE SPORTS
- Carbohydrate loading: 3–4 days before a race, increase carb intake to 8–12 g/kg/day and reduce training volume. Maximizes glycogen stores.
- During long events (>90 min): consume 30–60 g of carbohydrates per hour (gels, dates, sports drinks, banana)
- Fat adaptation: training in a glycogen-depleted state to improve fat oxidation efficiency; useful for ultra-endurance athletes

NUTRITION FOR RAMADAN ATHLETES
Training during Ramadan fasting is challenging. Recommendations:
- Train close to Iftar (just before breaking the fast) or 2–3 hours after Iftar when fueled
- Have a high-protein Suhoor meal to minimize muscle breakdown during the fast
- Prioritize complex carbs at Suhoor for sustained energy
- Hydrate maximally between Iftar and Suhoor
- Reduce training volume and intensity during Ramadan; maintain movement rather than chase PRs
