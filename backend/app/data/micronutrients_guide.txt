Micronutrients Guide: Vitamins and Minerals for Health and Performance

OVERVIEW
Micronutrients are vitamins and minerals required in small amounts for thousands of biochemical processes including energy production, immune function, hormone synthesis, bone maintenance, and muscle contraction. Unlike macronutrients, they provide no calories. Deficiencies are widespread globally and can profoundly affect energy levels, athletic performance, and long-term health even before clinical symptoms appear.

VITAMINS

VITAMIN A
Role: Vision (especially night vision), immune function, skin health, cell growth, reproduction.
Forms: Retinol (animal sources, preformed, directly used); Beta-carotene (plant sources, converted to vitamin A as needed).
Deficiency signs: Night blindness, frequent infections, dry skin, poor wound healing.
Sources: Liver (the richest source), eggs, dairy, orange and yellow vegetables (sweet potato, carrots), dark leafy greens (beta-carotene).
Daily target: 700 mcg (women), 900 mcg (men) RAE (Retinol Activity Equivalents).
Note: Excess preformed vitamin A (from supplements or liver consumed excessively) is toxic. Beta-carotene from plants is safe in excess.

VITAMIN B COMPLEX
The B vitamins are a group of 8 water-soluble vitamins involved in energy metabolism, nervous system function, and red blood cell production.

B1 (Thiamine): Converts carbohydrates to energy. Deficiency causes fatigue and neurological symptoms. Sources: whole grains, legumes, pork.

B2 (Riboflavin): Energy production, antioxidant function, growth. Deficiency: cracked lips, sore throat. Sources: dairy, eggs, meat, leafy greens.

B3 (Niacin): Energy metabolism, DNA repair, skin health. Large doses used therapeutically to raise HDL cholesterol. Sources: meat, fish, poultry, peanuts, whole grains.

B5 (Pantothenic Acid): Involved in all metabolic pathways; needed for hormone production. Widely found in food; deficiency is rare.

B6 (Pyridoxine): Protein metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis (serotonin, dopamine), immune function. Deficiency: depression, confusion, weakened immunity. Sources: chicken, fish, potatoes, bananas, nuts.

B7 (Biotin): Fatty acid synthesis, glucose metabolism, keratin production (hair, nails, skin). Sources: eggs, nuts, sweet potato, liver. Supplementation is unnecessary with adequate diet.

B9 (Folate/Folic Acid): DNA synthesis, cell division, amino acid metabolism. Critical in pregnancy to prevent neural tube defects. Deficiency: megaloblastic anemia. Sources: dark leafy greens (molokhia is exceptional), legumes, fortified cereals, avocado. Folic acid (supplement form) is more bioavailable than food folate.

B12 (Cobalamin): Myelin sheath formation (nerve insulation), red blood cell production, DNA synthesis. Found exclusively in animal products. Deficiency (very common in vegetarians/vegans): fatigue, nerve damage, megaloblastic anemia, cognitive decline. Sources: meat, fish, eggs, dairy. Vegans must supplement.

VITAMIN C
Role: Antioxidant, collagen synthesis (wound healing, skin, joint cartilage), immune function, iron absorption enhancement, neurotransmitter production.
Deficiency (scurvy): bleeding gums, poor wound healing, joint pain, fatigue — rare in modern diets.
Signs of suboptimal intake: poor wound healing, frequent infections, easy bruising.
Sources: citrus fruits, kiwi, bell peppers (highest vitamin C of any food), strawberries, broccoli, tomatoes, Egyptian karkadeh (hibiscus tea is rich in vitamin C).
Daily target: 75 mg (women), 90 mg (men). Smokers need an additional 35 mg/day.
Absorption: take iron supplements or eat iron-rich meals with vitamin C to maximize iron absorption.

VITAMIN D
Role: Calcium absorption (bone health), immune modulation, muscle function, hormone regulation (including testosterone), mood regulation.
Deficiency (extremely common globally; studies show deficiency rates of 60–80%+ in Egypt despite abundant sunlight, due to indoor lifestyle, sunscreen, and covered skin): rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults, fatigue, depression, frequent illness, muscle weakness, increased risk of many chronic diseases.
Sources: Sun exposure (primary source — 10–30 min of midday sun on bare skin), fatty fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel), egg yolks, fortified foods. Very few foods naturally contain vitamin D.
Daily target: The official RDA is 600 IU (adults) / 800 IU (over 70), but the Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guidelines (2023) recommend 1,000–2,000 IU/day for most adults to reliably achieve optimal blood levels. Higher doses (up to 4,000 IU/day) are considered safe and may be prescribed when levels are very low. The bare-minimum RDA is insufficient for most modern, indoor-living populations.
Testing: check 25-hydroxyvitamin D blood level. Target: 30–50 ng/mL.

VITAMIN E
Role: Powerful fat-soluble antioxidant; protects cell membranes from oxidative damage; immune function; skin health.
Deficiency: rare; nerve and muscle damage in severe cases.
Sources: nuts (almonds are the richest food source), seeds, vegetable oils (sunflower, wheat germ), avocado, leafy greens.
Daily target: 15 mg. Supplementation above 400 IU/day may increase bleeding risk.

VITAMIN K
K1 (Phylloquinone): Blood clotting. Sources: dark leafy greens, broccoli, Brussels sprouts.
K2 (Menaquinone): Bone and cardiovascular health — directs calcium to bones, keeps it from calcifying arteries. Sources: fermented foods, cheese, natto, animal products.
Daily target: 90 mcg (women), 120 mcg (men).
Note: People on blood thinners (warfarin) must maintain consistent vitamin K intake — sudden large increases interfere with medication.

MINERALS

IRON
Role: Hemoglobin synthesis (oxygen transport in blood), myoglobin in muscles, energy metabolism, immune function.
Deficiency (most common nutritional deficiency globally): fatigue, pallor, shortness of breath, impaired athletic performance, hair loss, brittle nails.
Daily target: 8 mg (men, postmenopausal women); 18 mg (premenopausal women); 27 mg (pregnant).
High-iron foods: liver/kibda (~6–20 mg/100g), red meat, shellfish (oysters highest), legumes, dark leafy greens.
See health conditions document for absorption optimization strategies.

CALCIUM
Role: Bone and teeth structure (99% of body calcium is in bones), nerve transmission, muscle contraction, blood clotting.
Deficiency: osteoporosis long-term; muscle cramps, tingling, dental problems.
Daily target: 1,000 mg (adults); 1,200 mg (women over 50, men over 70).
Sources: dairy products (highest bioavailability), fortified plant milks, sardines and canned salmon (with bones), white beans, broccoli, almonds.

MAGNESIUM
Role: 300+ enzyme reactions; energy production (ATP synthesis); protein synthesis; muscle and nerve function; blood glucose regulation; blood pressure regulation.
Deficiency: serum magnesium is a poor biomarker (only ~1% of body magnesium is in blood), so clinical deficiency rates appear low (2–15%), but functional/intracellular deficiency — undetectable by standard blood tests — is estimated to affect 40–50% of people eating processed-food-heavy diets. Symptoms of functional deficiency: muscle cramps, fatigue, poor sleep, anxiety, constipation, elevated blood pressure, impaired insulin sensitivity.
Daily target: 310–420 mg.
Sources: pumpkin seeds (highest), dark chocolate, almonds, spinach, black beans, avocado, whole grains.
Supplementation: magnesium glycinate (best absorbed; good for sleep and anxiety) or magnesium citrate (good for constipation).

ZINC
Role: Immune function, wound healing, protein synthesis, testosterone production, taste and smell, DNA synthesis.
Deficiency (common in populations with low meat intake): impaired immunity, poor wound healing, hair loss, loss of taste/smell, low testosterone, poor growth in children.
Daily target: 8 mg (women), 11 mg (men).
Sources: red meat and shellfish (oysters are the richest source), pumpkin seeds, legumes, nuts. Phytates in grains and legumes reduce zinc absorption — soaking and sprouting legumes improves bioavailability.

POTASSIUM
Role: Fluid balance, nerve signaling, muscle contraction (including heartbeat), blood pressure regulation (counteracts sodium).
Deficiency: muscle cramps, weakness, fatigue, heart arrhythmias (in severe cases).
Daily target: 2,600 mg (women), 3,400 mg (men).
Sources: bananas, sweet potato, potato, spinach, avocado, beans, yogurt, orange juice.

IODINE
Role: Thyroid hormone synthesis (T3 and T4), which regulate metabolism, growth, and brain development.
Deficiency: goiter (enlarged thyroid), hypothyroidism, cognitive impairment. Severe deficiency in pregnancy causes cretinism.
Daily target: 150 mcg (adults); 220 mcg (pregnant); 290 mcg (breastfeeding).
Sources: iodized salt, seafood, dairy, eggs.
Note: Use iodized salt. Most sea salt and Himalayan salt brands contain negligible iodine — check the label, as some premium brands now add iodine fortification, but most do not. Do not assume any salt other than labelled "iodized" contains iodine.

SELENIUM
Role: Antioxidant (component of glutathione peroxidase), thyroid hormone metabolism, immune function, reproductive health.
Deficiency: rare in most regions; associated with thyroid disorders and weakened immunity.
Daily target: 55 mcg. Upper limit: 400 mcg — excess causes toxicity.
Sources: Brazil nuts (1–2 per day provides the full daily requirement), tuna, sardines, shrimp, eggs, whole grains.

SODIUM
Role: Fluid balance, nerve signaling, muscle contraction.
Excess (most people consume too much): primary dietary driver of high blood pressure.
Daily limit: under 2,300 mg (about 1 teaspoon of salt); under 1,500 mg for hypertensive individuals.
Sources of excess sodium in diet: processed foods, canned goods, restaurant meals, condiments, bread, basterma, feseekh.

PHOSPHORUS
Role: Bone structure (with calcium), ATP energy molecule, cell membrane structure.
Deficiency: rare; excess (from processed food additives) may be harmful to kidney function.
Sources: dairy, meat, fish, eggs, nuts, legumes.

MICRONUTRIENT INTERACTIONS
- Vitamin D + Calcium: vitamin D is required for calcium absorption. Supplementing calcium without adequate vitamin D is ineffective.
- Iron + Vitamin C: enhances non-heme iron absorption significantly.
- Iron vs. Calcium: compete for absorption — take iron supplements separately from calcium-rich foods or supplements.
- Zinc vs. Iron: compete at high doses — avoid supplementing both simultaneously at high levels.
- Vitamin K + Blood Thinners: consistent intake essential (see above).
- B12 + Folate: B12 deficiency can be masked by high folate intake — always test both.
