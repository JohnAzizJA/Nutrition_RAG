Blood Sugar, Diabetes, and Dietary Management

UNDERSTANDING BLOOD SUGAR
Blood glucose (blood sugar) is the primary fuel for the brain and muscles. It comes from digested carbohydrates and is regulated by two hormones produced in the pancreas:
- Insulin: released when blood glucose rises, signals cells to absorb glucose; also promotes fat storage
- Glucagon: released when blood glucose falls, signals the liver to release stored glucose (glycogen)

Normal blood glucose ranges:
- Fasting (8+ hours without food): 70–99 mg/dL (3.9–5.5 mmol/L) — normal
- Fasting 100–125 mg/dL: pre-diabetes
- Fasting 126 mg/dL or above (on two separate tests): diabetes
- 2 hours after eating: under 140 mg/dL — normal
- HbA1c (3-month average): under 5.7% = normal; 5.7–6.4% = pre-diabetes; 6.5%+ = diabetes

HYPOGLYCEMIA (Low Blood Sugar)
Hypoglycemia is defined as blood glucose below 70 mg/dL. It is more common in people on insulin or certain diabetes medications but can affect anyone.

Symptoms: shakiness, sweating, dizziness, confusion, rapid heartbeat, hunger, pale skin, difficulty concentrating, anxiety, irritability. Severe hypoglycemia can cause loss of consciousness.

Causes: skipping meals, too much insulin or medication, intense exercise without adequate carbohydrate intake, excessive alcohol on an empty stomach.

Immediate treatment (15-15 rule): consume 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrates, wait 15 minutes, recheck blood sugar. If still low, repeat.
15g fast-acting carb sources:
- 4 glucose tablets
- 150 ml of fruit juice or regular soda (not diet)
- 1 tablespoon of sugar or honey
- Small piece of fruit

After recovery: eat a small balanced snack with complex carbs and protein to prevent recurrence.

Preventing hypoglycemia:
- Never skip meals, especially breakfast
- Eat carbohydrates before prolonged exercise; keep a fast-acting carb on hand during exercise
- Spread carbohydrate intake evenly across the day
- Avoid excessive alcohol, especially without food

Foods to eat for sustained blood sugar (preventing lows): oats, lentils, whole grain bread, sweet potato, Greek yogurt, nuts, eggs

HYPERGLYCEMIA (High Blood Sugar)
Hyperglycemia occurs when blood glucose is persistently elevated. In non-diabetics, the body compensates quickly. In diabetics or pre-diabetics, cells don't respond adequately to insulin.

Symptoms of chronically high blood sugar: frequent urination, excessive thirst, fatigue, blurred vision, slow wound healing, recurring infections, tingling in hands/feet (neuropathy).

GLYCEMIC INDEX AND GLYCEMIC LOAD
Glycemic Index (GI): ranks carbohydrate foods on a 0–100 scale based on how quickly they raise blood glucose.
- Low GI (55 or below): lentils, oats, most fruits, chickpeas, sweet potato, whole grain bread, Greek yogurt
- Medium GI (56–69): whole wheat bread, basmati rice, honey, raisins, new potatoes
- High GI (70 and above): white bread, white rice, cornflakes, watermelon, dates, sports drinks

Glycemic Load (GL) = (GI × grams of carbs per serving) / 100. A more practical measure because it accounts for portion size. GL under 10 is low; 11–19 is medium; 20+ is high.

Watermelon has a high GI but a low GL because a typical serving contains few actual carbs.

TYPE 2 DIABETES: DIETARY MANAGEMENT
Type 2 diabetes is characterized by insulin resistance — cells don't respond properly to insulin. Diet is the most powerful intervention.

Core principles:
1. Control total carbohydrate intake and choose low-GI sources
2. Distribute carbohydrates evenly across meals (avoid large carb loads in one sitting)
3. Increase fiber intake (25–40 g/day) — slows glucose absorption
4. Increase protein intake — has minimal effect on blood glucose and improves satiety
5. Reduce ultra-processed foods, added sugars, and refined carbs
6. Limit alcohol — can cause both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia; always consume with food
7. Achieve and maintain a healthy body weight — even 5–10% weight loss significantly improves insulin sensitivity

Recommended foods for Type 2 diabetes:
- Non-starchy vegetables: spinach, broccoli, cucumber, zucchini, peppers, tomatoes
- Legumes: lentils, chickpeas, black beans — high fiber, low GI
- Whole grains: oats, barley, quinoa, whole wheat
- Lean proteins: chicken, fish, eggs, Greek yogurt
- Healthy fats: olive oil, avocado, nuts, fatty fish
- Low-sugar fruits: berries, apples, pears, citrus (in controlled portions)

Foods to limit or avoid with diabetes:
- Sugar-sweetened beverages (soda, juice, energy drinks)
- White bread, white rice, regular pasta
- Pastries, cakes, cookies, sweets
- Fried foods and fast food
- High-GI foods in large portions. Note on fruit: whole dates, despite high sugar, have a low-to-medium glycemic load when consumed as whole fruit (fiber slows absorption) and are not equivalent to refined sugar — 2–3 whole dates is fine. Green (underripe) bananas have more resistant starch and a significantly lower GI (~42) than ripe bananas (~62); ripe bananas should be portioned carefully. Always choose whole fruit over juice.

The diabetes plate method: Fill half the plate with non-starchy vegetables, one quarter with lean protein, one quarter with complex carbohydrates.

TYPE 1 DIABETES
Type 1 is an autoimmune condition where the pancreas produces little or no insulin. Dietary management focuses on carbohydrate counting to match insulin doses. People with Type 1 diabetes must work closely with endocrinologists and dietitians. Exercise dramatically affects insulin sensitivity and must be planned with carbohydrate and insulin adjustments.

PRE-DIABETES
Pre-diabetes is fully reversible with lifestyle intervention. Studies show that losing 5–7% of body weight and walking 150 minutes per week can reduce progression to Type 2 diabetes by 58%.

RAMADAN FASTING AND BLOOD SUGAR
Muslims with diabetes face unique challenges during Ramadan. Key guidance:
- Work with a physician to adjust medication timing around Suhoor and Iftar
- Break the fast with dates (2–3) and water — provides fast-acting glucose without overloading
- Choose complex, slow-digesting carbs at Suhoor for sustained energy throughout the day
- Monitor blood glucose more frequently
- Stop fasting if blood glucose falls below 70 mg/dL or rises above 300 mg/dL

EXERCISE AND BLOOD SUGAR
- Aerobic exercise (walking, cycling, swimming) lowers blood glucose by increasing glucose uptake in muscles
- Resistance training improves long-term insulin sensitivity
- Monitor blood glucose before, during, and after exercise if diabetic
- Have fast-acting carbs available during exercise
- High-intensity exercise can temporarily raise blood glucose (due to stress hormone release) before it falls
