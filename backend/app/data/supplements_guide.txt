Supplements Guide: Evidence-Based Review for Fitness and Health

UNDERSTANDING SUPPLEMENTS
Supplements are meant to supplement — not replace — a well-structured diet and training program. The vast majority of supplements on the market have little to no scientific support. A small number have strong, consistent evidence. Money spent on whole food first; supplements second.

The hierarchy of priorities:
1. Consistent training with progressive overload
2. Calorie and protein targets from whole foods
3. Adequate sleep and recovery
4. Stress management
5. Supplements (after all above are addressed)

TIER 1: STRONG EVIDENCE — WORTH USING

CREATINE MONOHYDRATE
Evidence rating: 10/10 — the most researched supplement in existence
What it does: replenishes phosphocreatine stores in muscles, enabling more ATP production during short, maximal-effort activities (sprinting, heavy lifting). Over time: increases strength, power output, muscle mass, and training volume capacity.
Dosing: 3–5 g per day, every day. Timing doesn't matter. No loading phase needed (loading accelerates saturation by ~1 week but causes water retention and GI discomfort).
Safety: extremely safe for healthy individuals. Long-term use (years) shows no adverse effects on kidneys or liver in healthy people. Those with pre-existing kidney conditions should consult a physician.
Common myths: Does not meaningfully cause hair loss in >95% of users. One 2009 rugby study found DHT elevation (not hair loss) under an aggressive loading protocol — not replicated. Those with severe androgenetic alopecia predisposition may want to monitor, but no causal link is established. Does NOT cause dehydration when hydrated adequately. Does NOT cause weight gain beyond ~1 kg of intracellular water during initial saturation.
Form: creatine monohydrate. Other forms (HCl, ethyl ester, buffered) offer no advantages and cost more.

CAFFEINE
Evidence rating: 9/10
What it does: adenosine receptor antagonist — blocks the fatigue signal. Improves endurance, strength, power, focus, and reaction time.
Dosing: 3–6 mg per kg of body weight, 30–60 minutes before training. Example: 75 kg person = 225–450 mg (1–2 cups of strong coffee).
Side effects: insomnia (avoid within 8 hours of sleep), anxiety, increased heart rate, GI distress. Tolerance develops — cycling off 1–2 weeks every 2 months maintains efficacy.
Natural sources: coffee (~80–100 mg per 100ml), espresso (~60–80 mg per shot), tea (~30–50 mg per cup), pre-workout supplements.

PROTEIN SUPPLEMENTS (WHEY, CASEIN, PLANT-BASED)
Evidence rating: 8/10 as a convenient whole food substitute
What it does: provides dietary protein — no special anabolic properties beyond the protein itself.
Use when: total daily protein from whole foods is insufficient (below 1.6 g/kg). Convenient post-workout.
Whey: fast-digesting (2–3 hours), ideal post-workout. High in leucine (key anabolic trigger). Concentrate (80% protein), Isolate (90%+ protein, minimal lactose), Hydrolysate (pre-digested, faster but expensive).
Casein: slow-digesting (7 hours). Best before sleep. Keeps amino acids elevated overnight.
Plant-based: pea + rice combination most closely mimics whey's amino acid profile. Soy protein is also a complete protein. Good option for those with lactose intolerance or vegan diets.
Dosing: 20–40 g per serving. More than 40 g in a single dose provides no additional benefit for protein synthesis.

VITAMIN D3
Evidence rating: 9/10 for those deficient (which is most people)
What it does: most people in modern society — including in sun-rich countries like Egypt — are deficient due to indoor lifestyle, sunscreen, and covered skin. Vitamin D is involved in immune function, testosterone production, mood regulation, calcium absorption, and muscle function.
Dosing: 1,000–2,000 IU per day for maintenance. Higher doses (4,000–5,000 IU) may be prescribed if blood levels are very low. Take with a fat-containing meal for absorption.
Testing: check 25-hydroxyvitamin D blood level; target 30–50 ng/mL.
Form: D3 (cholecalciferol) is significantly more effective than D2 (ergocalciferol) at raising blood levels.

OMEGA-3 FATTY ACIDS (FISH OIL)
Evidence rating: 7/10 for cardiovascular and anti-inflammatory benefits
What it does: EPA and DHA (marine omega-3s) reduce inflammation, improve cardiovascular markers, support brain health, may modestly improve muscle protein synthesis and reduce soreness.
Dosing: 1–3 g of combined EPA+DHA per day. Take with meals to reduce fish burps.
Natural sources preferred: 2–3 servings of fatty fish per week (salmon, mackerel, sardines, herring) meets needs. Supplement if fish consumption is low.
Vegetarian alternative: algae oil contains EPA and DHA directly (fish get omega-3s from algae — going to the source). More expensive but effective.

MAGNESIUM
Evidence rating: 7/10 (for those with low dietary intake, which is common)
What it does: involved in 300+ enzyme reactions. Sleep quality, muscle relaxation, stress response, insulin sensitivity. Most processed-food-heavy diets are magnesium-deficient.
Dosing: 200–400 mg per day. Best taken at night.
Form: magnesium glycinate (best absorbed, best for sleep/anxiety), magnesium citrate (good for constipation), magnesium oxide (poorly absorbed — common cheap form, avoid).

TIER 2: MODERATE EVIDENCE — SITUATION-SPECIFIC

BETA-ALANINE
What it does: increases muscle carnosine levels, which buffers hydrogen ions during high-intensity exercise (2–4 minute effort range). May extend performance in events like 800m running, rowing, high-rep sets.
Dosing: 3.2–6.4 g per day. Split doses to reduce tingling (paresthesia). Takes 4+ weeks of loading to see effects.
Best for: combat sports, team sports, high-rep strength training, middle-distance running. Not particularly useful for powerlifting (1–5 rep work) or marathon running (different energy system).

CITRULLINE MALATE
What it does: precursor to arginine → nitric oxide; improves blood flow, reduces fatigue, may increase training volume. Better studied than arginine supplementation.
Dosing: 6–8 g of L-citrulline or 8–12 g of citrulline malate 30–60 minutes pre-workout.
Evidence: moderate; shows improvement in training volume (extra reps) and reduced muscle soreness.

COLLAGEN / VITAMIN C (FOR JOINTS)
What it does: collagen is a structural protein in tendons, ligaments, and cartilage. Supplementing hydrolyzed collagen with vitamin C before training may improve tendon collagen synthesis.
Dosing: 15 g hydrolyzed collagen + 50 mg vitamin C, 30–60 minutes before training sessions that stress tendons.
Evidence: emerging but promising, particularly for tendon injuries and joint pain.

ZINC
Useful if deficient (common in plant-heavy diets). Supports testosterone production, immunity, and protein synthesis. 25–45 mg/day. ZMA (zinc + magnesium + B6) is popular but there is limited evidence for enhanced testosterone in people with already-adequate levels.

TIER 3: WEAK EVIDENCE — GENERALLY NOT WORTH THE COST

BCAAs (Branched-Chain Amino Acids): leucine, isoleucine, valine. Useful only if training fasted and total protein intake is under 1.6 g/kg. If you eat adequate protein, BCAAs are redundant — whey protein already contains BCAAs. Not worth the cost for most people.

Glutamine: no meaningful benefit for muscle gain or recovery in people eating adequate protein. The gut uses most ingested glutamine before it reaches muscle.

HMB (Beta-hydroxy-beta-methylbutyrate): modest evidence in complete beginners or during severe caloric restriction. Not meaningful for trained athletes.

Testosterone boosters: most over-the-counter "test boosters" (tribulus, fenugreek) have minimal to no effect on testosterone levels in healthy men. Ashwagandha (KSM-66 or Sensoril extract, 300–600 mg/day) is an exception: multiple RCTs (2022–2023) show modest but consistent testosterone increases of 8–15% in men with elevated cortisol or high stress, along with improved sperm quality and reduced cortisol. Most effective in stressed individuals over 8+ week cycles.

Fat burners: most contain caffeine (which does increase metabolism) + other stimulants + unproven herbs. The caffeine effect is real but can be replicated by coffee at a fraction of the cost. Green tea extract (EGCG + caffeine) has modest fat oxidation evidence at high doses.

Pre-workout supplements: most are a combination of caffeine + beta-alanine + citrulline + B vitamins. You can replicate the evidence-based components independently for less money. Watch for proprietary blends that hide underdosed ingredients.

SUPPLEMENT SAFETY PRINCIPLES
- Research the brand: supplement industry is poorly regulated. Look for third-party testing certifications (Informed Sport, NSF Certified for Sport, USP verified)
- Start one supplement at a time to assess response and identify any adverse reactions
- More is not better — stick to evidence-based dosing
- Supplements cannot compensate for poor diet, inadequate training, or insufficient sleep
- Always inform your physician of any supplements you take — interactions with medications exist (fish oil + blood thinners, vitamin E + blood thinners, etc.)
- Athletes subject to anti-doping testing should use only certified supplements to avoid contamination with banned substances
