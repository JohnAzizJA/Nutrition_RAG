Dietary Management of Common Health Conditions

CARDIOVASCULAR DISEASE AND HIGH CHOLESTEROL

Understanding cholesterol:
- LDL (low-density lipoprotein): "bad" cholesterol; carries cholesterol to arteries; elevated levels increase cardiovascular risk
- HDL (high-density lipoprotein): "good" cholesterol; transports cholesterol away from arteries back to the liver for disposal; higher is better
- Triglycerides: a type of blood fat; elevated levels associated with cardiovascular disease; primarily driven by excess sugar, refined carbs, and alcohol

Target levels (adults):
- Total cholesterol: below 200 mg/dL
- LDL: below 100 mg/dL (below 70 for high-risk individuals)
- HDL: above 60 mg/dL
- Triglycerides: below 150 mg/dL

Dietary strategies to improve cholesterol:
1. Reduce saturated fat: limit red meat, butter, ghee, palm oil, full-fat dairy. Replace with olive oil, avocado, nuts.
2. Eliminate trans fats: avoid commercial fried foods, margarine, packaged pastries.
3. Increase soluble fiber: 5–10 g/day of soluble fiber lowers LDL by 5–11 points. Sources: oats, barley, apples, beans, lentils.
4. Increase omega-3 fatty acids: 2–3 servings of fatty fish per week (salmon, mackerel, sardines) raises HDL and reduces triglycerides.
5. Increase plant sterols: naturally occurring in nuts, seeds, whole grains. Block cholesterol absorption. 2 g/day reduces LDL by 8–10%.
6. Limit dietary cholesterol: eggs contain cholesterol but dietary cholesterol has less impact on blood cholesterol than previously thought for most people. Up to 7 eggs/week is fine for healthy individuals.
7. Reduce added sugar and refined carbs: major drivers of elevated triglycerides.
8. Increase exercise: aerobic exercise raises HDL and lowers triglycerides.

HYPERTENSION (HIGH BLOOD PRESSURE)
Normal blood pressure: below 120/80 mmHg
Elevated: 120–129 systolic
Stage 1 hypertension: 130–139 systolic or 80–89 diastolic
Stage 2 hypertension: 140+ systolic or 90+ diastolic
Hypertensive crisis: 180/120+ — seek immediate medical attention

The DASH Diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) is the gold standard dietary approach for managing blood pressure. It has been shown to reduce systolic blood pressure by 8–14 mmHg.

DASH diet principles:
- High in fruits and vegetables (8–10 servings per day)
- Whole grains instead of refined
- Low-fat dairy (2–3 servings per day)
- Lean proteins: fish, poultry, legumes
- Nuts and seeds: 4–5 servings per week
- Limit sodium to under 2,300 mg/day. Combining DASH with further sodium reduction to 1,500 mg/day achieves systolic BP reductions of 16–20 mmHg — greater than diet alone (Sacks et al. 2023 meta-analysis)
- Limit red meat, sweets, and sugary beverages

Sodium reduction strategies: cook with herbs and spices instead of salt, avoid processed and canned foods, taste food before adding salt, choose low-sodium products.

Potassium for blood pressure: potassium counteracts the effect of sodium on blood pressure. Target 3,500–4,700 mg/day. Sources: bananas, sweet potato, spinach, tomatoes, beans, avocado.

Magnesium: associated with lower blood pressure. Sources: dark leafy greens, nuts, seeds, whole grains, legumes.

Additional lifestyle factors: losing 5–10% body weight if overweight can lower systolic BP by 5–20 mmHg; aerobic exercise 30 min/day reduces BP by 4–9 mmHg; limiting alcohol; quitting smoking.

IRON DEFICIENCY ANEMIA
Anemia occurs when hemoglobin (iron-containing protein in red blood cells) is too low to carry adequate oxygen to tissues.

Symptoms: fatigue, pallor, shortness of breath, dizziness, cold hands and feet, brittle nails, cravings for non-food items (pica).

Iron requirements:
- Men: 8 mg/day
- Premenopausal women: 18 mg/day
- Pregnant women: 27 mg/day

Types of dietary iron:
- Heme iron (animal sources): absorbed at 15–35%. Found in red meat, liver, chicken, fish. Liver (kibda) is the richest source of iron at approximately 6–20 mg per 100g.
- Non-heme iron (plant sources): absorbed at 2–20%. Found in lentils, beans, spinach, fortified cereals, tofu.

Enhancing iron absorption:
- Eat vitamin C-rich foods with iron-rich meals (lemon juice on lentils, tomato with meat). Vitamin C converts ferric iron to ferrous form, which is better absorbed.
- Cook in cast iron pans — small amounts of iron leach into food.
- Avoid drinking tea or coffee with meals: tannins in tea reduce iron absorption by 60–70%. Wait 1 hour before/after iron-rich meals.
- Calcium (from dairy) and phytates (in whole grains and legumes) also inhibit iron absorption — spacing these from iron sources helps.

High-iron Egyptian foods: kibda (liver), basterma, kofta, molokhia, ful medames, shorbet ads.

THYROID CONDITIONS AND NUTRITION
Hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid): thyroid produces insufficient hormones. Causes fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin.

Dietary notes:
- Iodine is essential for thyroid hormone synthesis. Sources: iodized salt, seafood, eggs, dairy.
- Selenium supports thyroid function. Sources: Brazil nuts (1–2/day provides the full daily need), tuna, sardines.
- Zinc supports thyroid hormone production. Sources: red meat, shellfish, legumes, seeds.
- Goitrogenic foods (broccoli, cabbage, kale, soy): in theory interfere with thyroid function in very large raw quantities. Cooking neutralizes most goitrogens. Moderate consumption is safe.
- Do not take thyroid medication with calcium, iron supplements, or large amounts of fiber — impairs drug absorption.

Hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid): thyroid produces excess hormones. Causes weight loss, rapid heartbeat, heat intolerance, tremor, increased appetite.

POLYCYSTIC OVARY SYNDROME (PCOS)
PCOS is a hormonal condition affecting 8–13% of women of reproductive age, often associated with insulin resistance.

Dietary strategies for PCOS:
1. Reduce refined carbohydrates and added sugars (primary driver of insulin resistance)
2. Increase fiber intake (targets 25+ g/day)
3. Prioritize low-GI carbohydrates
4. Maintain adequate protein to support satiety and blood sugar stability
5. Include anti-inflammatory foods: fatty fish, olive oil, colorful vegetables, berries
6. Weight loss of 5–10% (in overweight women) significantly improves hormonal balance, ovulation regularity, and fertility
7. Inositol (found in whole grains, citrus fruits, nuts): now considered established evidence (multiple RCTs, endorsed by ASRM 2023). Myo-inositol at 2–4 g/day is evidence-based first-line therapy for improving insulin sensitivity, ovulatory function, and hormonal balance in PCOS

IRRITABLE BOWEL SYNDROME (IBS)
IBS affects bowel habits without structural disease. Common triggers vary between individuals.

Low-FODMAP diet: eliminates fermentable carbohydrates that cause gas, bloating, and altered bowel habits. Common high-FODMAP foods: onion, garlic, beans, wheat, dairy with lactose, certain fruits (apples, mango, watermelon). After symptom improvement, foods are reintroduced one at a time to identify personal triggers.

General IBS dietary principles:
- Eat regular meals; don't skip
- Limit caffeine, alcohol, and fatty foods
- Increase soluble fiber (oats, psyllium) for constipation-dominant IBS; reduce insoluble fiber for diarrhea-dominant
- Stay well hydrated
- Manage stress (strong gut-brain axis connection with IBS)

KIDNEY DISEASE
Dietary restrictions in kidney disease depend on disease stage (GFR). In advanced stages:
- Limit potassium: avoid bananas, oranges, potatoes, tomatoes, dairy
- Limit phosphorus: avoid processed foods, dark sodas, dairy, nuts
- Limit protein: contrary to general fitness advice, excessive protein strains failing kidneys
- Restrict sodium to under 2,000 mg/day
- Monitor fluid intake — kidneys cannot excrete excess fluid efficiently

Anyone with kidney disease must work with a nephrologist and registered renal dietitian for personalized guidance.

OSTEOPOROSIS AND BONE HEALTH
Osteoporosis is characterized by low bone density and fragility fractures. Nutrition plays a key preventive and therapeutic role.

Key nutrients:
- Calcium: 1,000 mg/day (adults under 50); 1,200 mg/day (women over 50, men over 70). Sources: dairy, fortified plant milk, sardines (with bones), leafy greens, white beans.
- Vitamin D: essential for calcium absorption. 600–800 IU/day (supplementation often needed in areas with low sunlight). Deficiency is extremely common in Egypt despite sun exposure (sunscreen and limited skin exposure).
- Vitamin K2: directs calcium into bones rather than arteries. Sources: fermented foods, cheese, animal products.
- Magnesium: necessary for calcium metabolism. Sources: nuts, seeds, whole grains, leafy greens.
- Protein: adequate protein intake supports bone matrix. Low protein diets are associated with lower bone density.

Bone-damaging habits: excessive alcohol, smoking, very low calcium intake, prolonged inactivity.
Weight-bearing exercise (resistance training, walking, running) directly stimulates bone density maintenance and growth.
