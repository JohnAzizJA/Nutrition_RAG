Macronutrients: Complete Guide to Proteins, Carbohydrates, and Fats

OVERVIEW
Macronutrients are the three primary nutrients that provide energy and building blocks for the body: protein, carbohydrates, and fats. Each gram of protein provides 4 calories, each gram of carbohydrate provides 4 calories, and each gram of fat provides 9 calories. Alcohol provides 7 calories per gram but is not a macronutrient. Understanding macronutrients is essential for achieving any nutrition or fitness goal.

PROTEIN
Protein is composed of amino acids and is the primary building material for muscle, skin, hair, enzymes, hormones, and immune cells. There are 20 amino acids; 9 are essential (must come from food).

How much protein do you need?
- Sedentary adults (general health): 0.8 g per kg of body weight per day
- Moderately active adults: 1.2–1.6 g/kg
- Regular strength trainers or athletes: 1.6–2.2 g/kg
- During a calorie deficit (cutting phase): up to 2.2–2.5 g/kg to preserve muscle mass
- Example: A 75 kg person doing resistance training needs approximately 120–165 g of protein per day

High-quality protein sources:
- Animal sources (complete proteins, containing all essential amino acids): chicken breast, turkey, eggs, fish (salmon, tuna, tilapia), lean beef, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, whey protein
- Plant sources: lentils, chickpeas, tofu, tempeh, edamame, quinoa, black beans. Most plant proteins are incomplete (missing one or more essential amino acids) but combining different plant foods throughout the day ensures adequate intake.

Protein timing:
- Spreading protein across 3–5 meals (25–40 g per meal) maximizes muscle protein synthesis compared to eating it all in one meal
- Consuming 20–40 g of protein within 2 hours after resistance training accelerates muscle repair and growth
- A slow-digesting protein (casein, found in cottage cheese or Greek yogurt) before bed can reduce overnight muscle breakdown

Signs of inadequate protein intake: muscle loss, slow wound healing, hair thinning, weakened immunity, persistent hunger.

CARBOHYDRATES
Carbohydrates are the body's preferred and most efficient energy source, particularly for the brain and during high-intensity exercise.

Types of carbohydrates:
1. Simple carbohydrates (fast-digesting): sugars found in fruit, dairy, and processed foods. They raise blood glucose quickly. Natural sources like fruit also provide fiber, vitamins, and minerals.
2. Complex carbohydrates (slow-digesting): found in whole grains, legumes, vegetables. They digest slowly, providing sustained energy and promoting satiety.
3. Dietary fiber: indigestible carbohydrate that feeds gut bacteria, regulates blood sugar, lowers cholesterol, and promotes fullness. Target: 25–38 g per day.

How much carbohydrate do you need?
- General recommendation: 45–65% of total daily calories
- Endurance athletes may need 55–70% of calories from carbs
- Low-carb diets (under 26% of calories or under 130 g/day) can be effective for weight loss or blood sugar control in some individuals
- Very low-carb/ketogenic diets (under 50 g/day) force the body into ketosis, burning fat for fuel. These require medical supervision for diabetics.

Glycemic Index (GI) and Glycemic Load (GL):
- GI measures how fast a food raises blood sugar (scale of 0–100)
- Low GI (under 55): oats, lentils, sweet potato, most fruits, whole grain bread
- High GI (over 70): white bread, white rice, sugary drinks, cornflakes
- GL accounts for both GI and portion size — a more practical tool for meal planning
- Choosing low-GI carbs generally improves satiety, blood sugar control, and sustained energy

Best carbohydrate sources: brown rice, oats, sweet potatoes, quinoa, lentils, chickpeas, whole wheat bread, fruits, vegetables.

Carbs to limit: white sugar, syrups, fruit juice, pastries, white bread, processed snacks.

FATS
Fat is essential for hormone production (including testosterone and estrogen), brain function, fat-soluble vitamin absorption (A, D, E, K), cell membrane integrity, and joint lubrication.

Types of fat:
1. Unsaturated fats (healthy): monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats. Found in olive oil, avocado, nuts, seeds, fatty fish. Reduce LDL cholesterol and inflammation.
2. Saturated fats (limit): found in red meat, butter, cheese, coconut oil, palm oil. Raise LDL cholesterol when consumed in excess. Limit to under 10% of total calories.
3. Trans fats (avoid): industrially produced through hydrogenation. Found in margarine, fried fast food, packaged pastries. Raise LDL and lower HDL — no safe level of consumption.
4. Omega-3 fatty acids: a type of polyunsaturated fat with powerful anti-inflammatory properties. Found in fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), flaxseed, walnuts, chia seeds. Target 1,000–2,000 mg EPA+DHA daily for cardiovascular and anti-inflammatory benefit; the minimum for deficiency prevention is 250–500 mg, but current guidelines (AHA 2023, Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics 2021) recommend higher intake for meaningful health effects.

How much fat do you need?
- Minimum: 15% of total daily calories (for hormone function)
- Recommended range: 20–35% of total daily calories
- Example: on a 2000 kcal diet, 44–78 g of fat per day
- Very low-fat diets (under 15%) can impair hormone production and fat-soluble vitamin absorption

FIBER
Dietary fiber is technically a carbohydrate but deserves special attention:
- Soluble fiber (oats, apples, legumes): dissolves in water, slows glucose absorption, lowers LDL cholesterol, feeds beneficial gut bacteria
- Insoluble fiber (whole grains, vegetables): adds bulk to stool, prevents constipation, reduces colorectal cancer risk

Daily fiber targets: 25 g for women, 38 g for men; most people consume only 15 g/day.

TRACKING MACROS
- To lose weight: create a calorie deficit of 300–500 kcal/day; prioritize high protein (2.0–2.5 g/kg) to preserve muscle
- To gain muscle: calorie surplus of 200–400 kcal/day; 1.6–2.2 g/kg protein; adequate carbs to fuel training
- To maintain weight: match calories to TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure); balanced macros
- Tracking tools: food diary apps, food scales for accuracy
- Flexible dieting (IIFYM — If It Fits Your Macros): focuses on hitting daily macro targets rather than food restriction; evidence supports it for body composition goals when calories are controlled

HYDRATION AND MACROS
Water is not a macronutrient but affects nutrient absorption. Protein digestion increases water needs. A general starting point is 35–45 ml per kg of body weight daily; however, ACSM 2023 guidance emphasizes individualized hydration based on sweat rate, climate, and training intensity rather than fixed formulas. Urine color (target pale yellow) is the most practical daily hydration indicator.
